The Only Hope: A Sea of Dreams and Ghosts

A Journey Across the Mediterranean
In 2017, my neighbor's daughter disappeared. One day, Ajoke was selling akara by the roadside, laughing in that way that made her whole body shake. The next day, she was just... gone. No note. No explanation. Just an empty bed and a pair of slippers left neatly by the door.
Image: A pair of slippers by an empty bed
The beginning of disappearance

Her parents searched—oh God, how they searched. They called every relative, visited every friend, even went to churches and babalawos. For three years, Ajoke was a ghost in our conversations, a painful pause in her mother's laughter, a phantom shape in the doorway every evening when the streetlights flickered on.

Then, a WhatsApp message appeared. Grainy, pixelated. A photo of Ajoke standing in front of what looked like a train station, snow on the ground, her smile thin and unfamiliar. The caption read: "Mama, I reach Italy. I am safe." Safe. After three years of silence, that was the word she used. What happened in those three years? The journey came in pieces, broken sentences sent in the dead of night: the desert that stole breath, Libya that stole dignity, and the sea—the Mediterranean—that nearly stole everything.

Ajoke's story is not her own. Walk through the streets of Benin, Edo State, and you'll hear it whispered like a prayer. "He has gone to Italy." Not Europe. Not abroad. Italy. The word is said with a particular reverence, a capital letter in the middle of a sentence. Itali. It rolls off the tongue not as a place, but as a promise. The Only Hope. A land where roads are paved with opportunity, where jobs wait on street corners, where suffering is exchanged for visas at the border. It is a dream sold in living rooms by smooth-talking "travel agents," packaged with lies and financed by sold land, pawned jewelry, and collective family hope.

The Leaving

Image: A quiet goodbye at night
The silent departure

The decision to leave doesn't happen in a moment. It ferments. It grows in the stomach of a graduate driving a keke for 14 hours a day. It whispers to the tailor whose shop was washed away in the last rains. It shouts at the young man watching his friends picked up by soldiers and never returning. The push isn't just poverty; it's the slow, suffocating collapse of possibility. The future isn't just bleak; it's a wall.

So they go. They sell the family plot—the one their great-grandfather farmed. The mother gives her gold, the last physical memory of her own mother. The money is counted, folded, handed over to a man in sharp shoes who promises "safe passage." The goodbye is not dramatic. It is a quiet hug, averted eyes, because to look too long would be to see the terror and maybe, maybe turn back. They leave at night. They become ghosts before they ever reach the sea.

The Desert: Where Bones Blossom

Image: The vast, unforgiving Sahara Desert
The desert that consumes dreams

The Sahara is not empty. It is full of ghosts. Our guides told us—the ones who came back broken—that the desert blooms with bones. Shoes, mostly. Single, weathered sneakers marking trails where people walked until they could not. The heat is a physical violence. It doesn't just burn; it consumes. Throat, eyes, mind. People share water until the bottles are licked dry. When the pickup truck breaks down—and it always breaks down—the "connection man" on the phone says, "Walk. North."

Some are left behind. Not out of cruelty, but out of a brutal, silent arithmetic of survival. You cannot carry a man who cannot stand when your own legs are trembling mirages. You weep as you walk away. You promise to send help. You never can. The desert swallows signals, promises, people. By the time you reach Libya, your humanity is already cracked. The desert has scraped you raw.

Libya: The Factory of Broken Men

Image: A crowded detention center in Libya
The factory of broken dreams

If the desert tries to kill you, Libya tries to unmake you. This is the open secret, the hellish purgatory in the brochure. You are not a traveler here. You are currency. You are grabbed, sold from one militia to another. You work—forced labor—to pay for your own captivity. The stories that filter back are not stories; they are wounds. Beatings for sport. Ransom calls to your weeping mother back in Nigeria, her voice tinny and shattered on the line. Crammed into warehouses where the air is thick with sweat, waste, and despair.

Here, the Mediterranean, which you have never seen, begins to glitter in your mind not as a danger, but as a deliverance. The sea becomes the only door out of the factory. You will risk the waves to escape the concrete. The decision is made in a dark cell: death by water is preferable to death by slow erasure.

The Crossing: Prayers in a Rubber Dinghy

Image: A crowded rubber dinghy on rough seas
The gamble with the sea

You see it finally, at night. The Mediterranean. It is vast and black, merging with the sky. It does not look like water; it looks like oblivion. The boat is a joke. A flimsy rubber dinghy meant for 20 now holds 120 souls. You pay extra for a life jacket—a cheap, orange foam thing that you pray you never have to test. The engine sputters. The "captain" is a boy from Ghana who looks as terrified as you feel.

You push off from the sand. For the first hour, there is silence, punctuated only by the putter of the engine and the sound of vomiting. The boat sits so low, every small wave kisses the rim. When the first real swell comes, a collective gasp sucks the air out of the boat. Water sloshes at your ankles. Then your calves. Panic is a smell—sharp, metallic. Someone begins to pray in a language you don't understand. Another sings a hymn. The sound is swallowed by the wind.

This is the gamble. Will the steel-hearted sea be merciful? Will the coast guard come? Or will a rogue wave, a single puncture, turn this rubber into a tomb? You think of your mother. You bargain with God. You promise everything. The hours stretch. Night becomes a grey, nauseating dawn. When you finally see the ship—the large, clean vessel with MSF or Sea-Watch painted on the side—you don't cheer. You weep. A deep, body-shaking weep of relief and grief for the part of you that died on the journey.

Survival Italy: The Bitter Green

Image: A migrant reception center in Italy
The reality of arrival

You arrive. You are processed, fingerprinted, given a foil blanket that crinkles like laughter. You are taken to a centro di accoglienza—a reception center. It is clean, and that itself is a shock. But the dream quickly hardens into a new reality.

The "greener pasture" is a crowded dormitory. It is paperwork in a language you cannot read. It is waiting. Waiting for the permesso di soggiorno. Waiting for a work permit. Waiting for a "yes" or a "no" that will decide your entire life. You hear stories from those who came before you: the Nigerian man with a PhD cleaning toilets for cash; the woman from Ghana paying 500 euros a month to sleep in a windowless basement; the racism that is not shouted, but whispered in cold service and closed doors.

You call home. "Mama, I am fine. Italy is fine." You paint a picture of the good life. You send a few euros via Western Union—the digital proof of your success. You hang up and stare at the wall of your temporary room. The ghost of the Sahara is in your dry skin. The ghost of Libya is in your jumpy nerves. The ghost of the sea is in your dreams, where you are always drowning.

You realize the truth: you didn't cross a sea. You exchanged one struggle for another. The "green" here is the color of survival, not prosperity. The pasture is fenced with bureaucracy, loneliness, and the crushing weight of expectation—the expectation of everyone back home who thinks you have made it.

Is it really greener on the other side?

Is It Greener?

Image: A person looking at two different landscapes
The eternal question

So we return to Ajoke's question, to the question etched on every face that boards those doomed dinghies: Is it really greener on the other side?

For the ones who did not make it—the thousands whose names are recorded only as "unknown migrant" in Italian ledgers, whose bodies are buried in numbered graves in Lampedusa, or who simply vanished into the deep—the answer is a silent, blue void.

For the ones who made it, the answer is a daily negotiation. The green is in the safety from a bullet. It is in the euro that can buy medicine for a sick parent. It is in the chance, however slim, to build something. But it is tinged with the grey of nostalgia, the brown of discrimination, and the deep blue of the sea they can never forget.

The grass is not greener. It is different grass, growing on harder, colder soil. And it is watered with a price that can never be counted in currency, only in memories, trauma, and the perpetual feeling of being straddled between two worlds, belonging to neither.

The Unending Tide

Image: Waves crashing on the shore
The unending cycle

Ajoke is still in Italy. She works in an elderly care home. She sends money every month. Her parents have rebuilt their house. From the outside, it is a success story. But when I spoke to her last, her voice was thin over the phone. "Tell them not to come," she said, a desperate whisper. "Unless there is truly no hope at all. Tell them the sea has mercy for no one."

But they will still come. Because when the back is against the wall, a slim chance in a distant land will always look brighter than no chance at home. The Mediterranean will continue to be what it has become: a liquid border, a final judge, a cemetery of dreams, and for a desperate few, the only hope left on earth.

The tide, it seems, is unending. And so is the hope. And so are the ghosts.

About the Writer
This piece was written by someone who has witnessed the empty spaces left behind in Nigerian communities—the vacant chairs at dinner tables, the hushed conversations about missing sons and daughters, the Western Union receipts that tell only half the story. The writer has listened to the fragments of journeys from those who returned broken and those who made it but carry the weight of the crossing in their silence. This is not just a story; it is a collection of truths gathered from the echoes of countless departures and arrivals.
